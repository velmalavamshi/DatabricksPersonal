# Hologic Punctuation Reference (AP style, full detail)

Source: Hologic Brand Center Editorial Style Guide (styleguide.hologic.com), the actual docx
export. This expands on the quick-reference table in `terminology.md` and `SKILL.md` — pull
this file in only for content where punctuation precision actually matters (legal-adjacent
copy, long-form documents, anything getting close proofing) rather than routine writing.

Where this file and the quick-reference disagree, this file wins — it's the fuller, directly
sourced version.

## Apostrophes
- Plural nouns not ending in s: add **'s** — *the alumni's contributions*.
- Plural nouns ending in s: add only an apostrophe — *the churches' needs*.
- Singular common nouns ending in s: add **'s** — *the hostess's invitation*.
- Singular proper names ending in s: apostrophe only — *Dickens' novels* (exception: *St.
  James's Palace*).
- No apostrophe in possessive pronouns — *its, whose, theirs* — but watch for genuine
  contractions (*it's, who's*).
- Compound words: apostrophe goes on the word closest to the object possessed — *the attorney
  general's request*.
- Joint possession: *Fred and Sylvia's apartment*. Individual: *Fred's and Sylvia's books*.
- Descriptive phrases (no possession implied) skip the apostrophe — *a teachers college* (test:
  does "for" or "by" fit the longer form?).
- Quasi possessives: *two weeks' vacation* — but no apostrophe when quantity precedes an
  adjective: *six months pregnant*.
- Omitted letters/figures: *rock 'n' roll*, *the '60s*.
- Plurals of single letters: *mind your p's and q's* — but no apostrophe for plurals of numerals
  or multi-letter combos.

## Colons
- Capitalize the word after a colon only if it's a proper noun or starts a complete sentence.
- Use to introduce lists, tabulations, or texts.
- Comma introduces a one-sentence direct quote; colon introduces longer quotations.
- Colons go outside quotation marks unless part of the quotation itself.
- Never combine a dash and a colon.

## Commas
- No comma before the conjunction in a simple series — *red, white and blue* — unless omitting
  it would create ambiguity, or a series element itself contains "and": *orange juice, toast,
  and ham and eggs*.
- Use commas between coordinate adjectives (*a thoughtful, precise manner*) but not when the
  final adjective is integral to the noun phrase (*a cheap fur coat*).
- Set off nonessential clauses/phrases with commas; don't set off essential ones.
- Comma before and/but/for linking two independent clauses with their own subjects; skip it when
  the subject is shared.
- Comma (not period) before attribution after a quote — *"Write clearly," she said.*
- Hometowns/ages: *Maude Findlay, 48, Tuckahoe, New York, was present.*
- Commas always go inside quotation marks.
- Full dates: set off the year with a comma when month, day, and year are all present — *Feb.
  14, 2029, is the target date.*

## Dashes
- Em dash (—), never en dash, for abrupt change in thought or emphatic pause — space on both
  sides. Avoid overuse where a comma would do.
- Use dashes to set off a phrase that itself contains a comma-separated series.
- Hyphens (no surrounding spaces) are for compound modifiers and ranges only — *small-business
  owner*, *Jan. 1-4*.

## Ellipses
- Treat as a three-letter word: three periods, two spaces (in traditional AP typesetting) or per
  house convention. Never open or close a direct quote in running text with one.
- If the text before the ellipsis is a complete sentence, punctuate the sentence first, then
  add the ellipsis.

## Exclamation Points
- Reserve for real surprise or strong emotion — avoid overuse.
- Inside quotation marks when part of the quoted material; outside when not.
- Never combine with a comma or period.

## Hyphens
- Use when it aids clarity, skip when it adds clutter — *he recovered his health* vs. *he
  re-covered the leaky roof*.
- Hyphenate compound modifiers before a noun (*low-income workers*) but often not after (*she
  works full time*) — except where dropping it would confuse (*blast-cleaned*).
- No hyphen in phrasal verbs (*back up the car*), but do hyphenate other compound verbs
  (*speed-walked*).
- Don't hyphenate dual heritage — *African American*, *Mexican American*.
- Suspensive hyphenation: *10-, 15- or 20-minute intervals*.

## Parentheses
- Needing them is often a sign a sentence is contorted — try commas or dashes first.
- Period goes outside the closing parenthesis unless the parenthetical is itself a full,
  independent sentence.
- Never use parentheses to denote a political figure's party/jurisdiction — use commas.

## Periods
- End declarative/mildly imperative sentences with a period.
- Initials: *John F. Kennedy* — but initials-only abbreviations skip periods (*JFK*).
- Periods always go inside quotation marks. Single space after a sentence-ending period.

## Question Marks
- Use for direct questions, including declarative-form questions (*You started the riot?*).
- Do not use to end an indirect question (*He asked who started the riot.*)
- Placement with quotation marks depends on meaning — inside if the question is part of the
  quote, outside if it applies to the whole sentence.

## Quotation Marks
- Running quotations across paragraphs: omit closing marks at the end of each paragraph except
  the last; open each new paragraph with opening marks.
- Quotes within quotes: alternate double and single marks.
- Period and comma always go inside quote marks; dash/semicolon/colon/question mark/exclamation
  point go inside only when they apply to the quoted material itself.
- Headlines use single quote marks.

## Semicolons
- Use to clarify a series when items are long or contain their own commas — including before
  the final "and."
- Use to link two independent clauses when no coordinating conjunction is present.
- Always placed outside quotation marks.
